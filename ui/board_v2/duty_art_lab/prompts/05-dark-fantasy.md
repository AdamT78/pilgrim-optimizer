TWO REFERENCE IMAGES, EACH FOR ONE THING ONLY
======================================================================

REFERENCE 1 - THE GREY GROUND PLATE - IS THE CAMERA.

Take from it only the approximately 32 degree elevated oblique viewing angle,
the tabletop board-game viewpoint, the orthographic or very long lens, the
clear readable ground plane, and the cut-away convention where roofs and near
walls are removed so the camera can see in. Nothing else.

REFERENCE 2 - THE WIDE MEDIEVAL SCENE - IS THE HAND.

Take from it only HOW IT IS DRAWN: the weight and density and direction of the
cross-hatching, how much is described by line and how much by tone, how stone
and worn timber and coarse cloth and skin are each described, the degree of
finish, the restraint of the faces.

DO NOT TAKE ITS COLOUR, and do not take its mood either. Reference 2 is calm.
This picture is not. Match its ink and its craft; ignore its temperature and
its temperament.

Take nothing else from reference 2: not its subject, buildings, props, figures,
staging or composition. Only the hand that drew it.

======================================================================
THE REGISTER: GRIM DARK FANTASY, NOT AN ILLUSTRATED CHRONICLE
======================================================================

Fourteenth-century England as a place of dread. Everything in it is real -
there are no monsters, no magic, no invented creatures - and it is terrifying
anyway, because the institutions are vast and old and indifferent and the
people are small and cold and have no choice.

The lineage is the dark nineteenth-century engraving: Gustave Dore's infernal
plates, Piranesi's prisons. Enormous architecture, deep blacks, light used
sparingly and hard, figures dwarfed by the thing they are inside.

What that is NOT: heroic fantasy, armour and banners, a battle, a monster, a
glowing artefact, a cinematic hero shot. Nothing supernatural. The horror here
is institutional and physical - cold stone, disease, obedience, and the weight
of a building that will outlast everyone in it.


======================================================================
THE MOMENT, NOT THE CEREMONY
======================================================================

Do not draw an event. Draw the one second in it that decides everything.

Every scene is caught mid-action, at the top of its movement:

- one gesture at FULL EXTENSION - an arm out straight, a hand coming down, a
  head thrown back, a body at the limit of its reach
- one thing in motion that could not hold still: cloth caught mid-swing,
  smoke rolling, rain, a cast shadow sweeping, birds going up
- at least one figure OFF BALANCE - turning, stumbling, kneeling hard,
  recoiling, gripping something to stay upright

Compose the action on a DIAGONAL across the card, not parallel to its edges.
The strongest line in each card should run corner to corner, not left to right.

Faces carry the scene. At least one face must be fully readable and doing
something specific - not solemn, but frightened, exhausted, pitiless or
resolute. Solemnity is what the last version had and it is what makes a card
look like a woodcut of a committee.


======================================================================
SCALE IS THE DRAMA
======================================================================

The figures stay small. That is not a limitation to work around, it IS the
effect: a person is frightening to look at when the room is enormous.

So let the architecture be enormous. Columns that leave the frame at the top.
Vaulting so high the camera never finds its springing. A door built for
something larger than a man. Depth going back further than the eye expects.

Give every card a clear foreground, middle and far distance. Put something
close and dark at one lower corner - a stone edge, a rail, a shoulder - so the
eye enters past it. The last version was all at one depth and read flat.


======================================================================
LIGHT IS THE SUBJECT
======================================================================

THIS IS THE BIGGEST CHANGE AND THE ONE MOST LIKELY TO BE IGNORED.

Stop lighting with flat even overcast. Measured across what this set has
produced so far, barely half a percent of any picture is brighter than a light
mid-grey - there is no highlight anywhere in it, and that is why it reads
dutiful rather than dreadful.

Light this with ONE or TWO hard sources and let everything else fall away:

- a shaft coming down through a high opening, raking across the floor
- a brazier or a bank of candles, low and close, lighting faces from beneath
- a doorway blazing behind a figure so it reads as a black silhouette

Around 3 to 6 per cent of each card should be genuinely bright - a lit face, a
flame and its immediate pool, a struck edge of stone, a wet flagstone catching
the shaft. Not a wash. Hard-edged, small, and placed where it does the work.

Everything else goes deep. Blacks are rich and RETAIN THEIR HATCHING - no
large area collapses into flat empty black, and detail stays separable in the
shadows. The contrast is between a small brilliant thing and a vast dark one.

Keep flames small, simple in silhouette, against darker ground, with
uncluttered space around them, and never across a face's key features or the
central gesture - they may be animated later.

No god rays as decoration, no lens flare, no glow around people, no modern
colour grading, no teal-and-orange.


======================================================================
THE MIDDLE IS A VEIL
======================================================================

Nothing stands on the centre line: no pier, column, post, tree, gate or
banner, and no person, face, animal or object that could be named.

The middle tenth of the width is the DEEPEST and SOFTEST part of the picture -
where smoke, incense, dust, rain or cold mist swallows the far end of the
space. Not bare ground: a veil. It reads as depth rather than as emptiness,
which is what lets the two scenes sit close to it without a hole between them.

NO BRIGHT THING NEAR THE CENTRE. This matters more here than in any earlier
version, because this brief asks for hard light: a flame, a shaft or a lit
face within the middle fifth of the width will put a hard edge exactly where
the picture is cut. Keep every light source well out towards the sides.

THE LEVEL RULE STILL HOLDS. A narrow strip is removed from the middle when the
picture is cut. An edge that is LEVEL where it crosses the centre stays level
and the cut cannot be seen; an edge that SLOPES there breaks into a visible
step - about three finished pixels at ten degrees, nine at thirty, on a card
only a hundred and eighty tall. So the ground line, and any wall top, roof
line, rail or step edge crossing the middle, must be within a few degrees of
level there, or must not be there at all. A diagonal wall or roof across the
centre is the single worst thing you can put in this picture.


======================================================================
FIGURE SCALE
======================================================================

Judge against a FINISHED CARD, not the whole master: the top and bottom of the
master are overscan and will be gone.

A STANDING ADULT IS BETWEEN 55% AND 70% OF THE FINISHED CARD'S HEIGHT.

Each group, all its figures together, occupies roughly the middle 60% of its
card's width, with clear ground below the lowest foot. Three to five figures a
side. Avoid crowds; a mass of people may appear as a dark undifferentiated
bank behind the readable ones, and counts as architecture, not as figures.

======================================================================
CAMERA - UNCHANGED, AND NOT NEGOTIABLE
======================================================================

Elevated oblique board-game camera, approximately 32 degrees above the ground
plane. Orthographic or a very long lens approximating it, with restrained and
consistent convergence across the whole width.

All of these still hold:
- the ground plane fills roughly the lower two thirds
- the horizon is ABOVE the top edge; no sky is visible anywhere
- the camera sees the TOP SURFACES of steps, thresholds, tables and walls
- the camera sees the tops of heads and shoulders of standing figures
- a wide patch of ground is visible around every figure's feet
- the same downward angle and oblique direction everywhere

Drama comes from light, scale, gesture and depth. It does NOT come from moving
the camera. No eye level, no low hero angle, no wide angle, no foreground
enlargement, no tilted horizon, no second vanishing point for the right half.


======================================================================
DRAWING
======================================================================

Hand-coloured engraving over dense cross-hatching, bitten deep. Fine etched
line structure and tonal modelling, never smooth digital gradients. The
hatching stays readable everywhere, including in the deepest shadow. Physically
printed, not photographic, not painterly, not airbrushed.

Weather-stained stone, worn flagstones, ancient masonry, gothic arches, dark
timber, iron fittings, rough stone and turf and wattle where the poor live,
historically grounded clothing and furnishings. No fantasy armour, no invented
heraldry, no ornament that a fourteenth-century mason would not recognise.


======================================================================
COLOUR
======================================================================

The overall warmth is set afterwards and is not this picture's job. What IS
its job is to stay out of the way of that:

- the picture reads as a near-monochrome engraving with one warm accent
- the warm accent belongs ONLY to fire and lamplight and what they touch
- NO blue, green, red, violet or saturated orange anywhere
- no coloured clothing, no coloured heraldry
- timber, earth, turf, thatch and leather are grey, not brown

Put the drama in VALUE, not in hue. A picture that is nearly black and white
and has one small blazing light in it is the target. A picture that reaches for
colour to feel dramatic has missed.


======================================================================
BUILT TO BE SEEN SMALL
======================================================================

Each finished card is drawn at roughly a third of a postcard. Bold readable
silhouettes, strong value grouping, large clear gestures, a simple focal
hierarchy, and hard separation of subject from background. If the main action
is not legible as a shape alone, it has failed, however fine the detail is.


======================================================================
EXCLUSIONS
======================================================================

No caption, label, lettering, number, action name, UI, button, seal, icon,
border, frame, parchment separator or panel divider. No sky, horizon, distant
landscape, village or rooftops. No castle rampart, tower or fortress. No
monsters, demons, skeletons, ghosts, magic, glowing eyes or supernatural
effects of any kind. No modern objects. No gore.


======================================================================
PRIORITIES, IN ORDER
======================================================================

1.  the stated aspect ratio, frame filled
2.  ONE or TWO hard light sources, roughly 3-6% of each card genuinely bright,
    everything else deep and still hatched - and NO bright thing near the
    centre line
3.  the decisive moment: one gesture at full extension, one thing in motion,
    one figure off balance, composed on a diagonal
4.  enormous architecture dwarfing small figures; clear foreground, middle and
    far distance
5.  the middle tenth is a veil of smoke or mist, and every edge crossing it is
    level
6.  a standing adult 55-70% of the finished card's height
7.  everything indispensable inside the vertical safe band
8.  32 degree tabletop camera, no sky, one vanishing behaviour
9.  two complete compositions, each readable alone
10. grim engraving, near-monochrome, drama in value and not in hue
11. legible at small size
