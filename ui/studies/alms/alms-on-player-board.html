Putting the alms table on the player boards

Two facts have to survive: where each player stands on the 0–6 track, which resets after each of the first three seasons, and how many acolytes they already have on the alms table. Everything else the table draws — the reward wording, the season columns, which season each win came from — is reference, not state.

From configs/alms.json: the track runs 0–6 and pays at 2, 4 and 6 (serf to abbey, acolyte to city, serf to city). Acolytes on the table score 0 / 5 / 11 / 18 / 26, so the fourth is worth 8 on its own — the count is not a linear thing and it is worth showing exactly. Shown here: Red 5 with 2 wins, Yellow 0 with none, Blue 3 with 1, White 6 with 3.

V1 — footer rail

Cost: 24 px of height per card, 96 for the column.

The whole track, drawn: seven cells 0–6 with the reward steps (2, 4, 6) in gold, the seat's disc on the current one, and the reset said in words. Wins sit under the portrait as filled and empty cubes, with the score they are currently worth.

Nothing is inferred and nothing is hidden — you can see how far to the next reward, which is the question the track exists to answer.

V2 — a fourth column

Cost: 74 px of width per card, so the left column goes 352 → 426.

Two more pills in the grammar the card already speaks: a number with a glyph. The alms glyph is the track itself, seven rungs with the gold ones at 2, 4 and 6 and the seat's bar on the current rung; the wins glyph is a 2×2 tray of acolytes.

Reads the same way as everything else on the card, which is its whole argument. But width is the dimension the table has least of, and this spends it on the least-used numbers.

V3 — the collar is the track

Cost: nothing. Same card, same 320 × 112.

The collar widens from 7 to 15 and becomes the track: seven notches around the portrait's north-west arc, gold at 2, 4 and 6, with the position ridden by a disc carrying the number. Wins are gold stars on the south-west arc — count them, no number needed.

The risk is legibility: this is the smallest of the three and it puts two unrelated facts on one ring. Check White and Yellow, where the collar is pale and the gold has least to push against.
