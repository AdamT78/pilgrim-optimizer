KEEP EVERYTHING GEOMETRIC EXACTLY AS IT IS IN THE ATTACHED IMAGE — the camera, the
ellipse it draws, the rim's thickness and the way that rim is foreshortened, the size
in frame, the flat orthographic projection with no converging joint lines.

ONE LIMIT, AND IT IS A MEASUREMENT, NOT AN IMPRESSION: the ellipse of the paved
surface must never measure more than 0.530 of its width. Measure it on your own
output before you finish, on the paving inside the rim, not counting the rim's
thickness. Past that the camera has climbed too high and the tile is wrong, however
good the stone looks.

CHANGE ONLY THE STONE. Warm pale limestone, weathered and pitted, laid as large
rectangular slabs in a regular bond with staggered joints — eight to twelve slabs
across the whole tile, not thirty. Tight joints, very little mortar. A few of the
slabs cracked. Grim and muted, 14th century England. Light from the upper left, as in
the attached image. Nothing glossy or wet.

THE MEASURING RING:
Draw a single closed ellipse in pure spring green, RGB 0 255 128, as a thin line
around the tile — about 2% of the tile's width thick. It is a measuring mark, not
part of the scene: flat colour, no texture, no shading, no glow.

The ring must be the SAME ellipse the tile is: a circle lying flat on the same
ground, seen from the same camera, so its height is the same fraction of its width
as the tile's own paved surface.

The ring must be clearly larger than the tile and must not touch or pass behind it
anywhere — leave an obvious gap all the way round, with the ring about half again as
wide as the tile. The whole ring must be visible and unbroken.

Transparent background, exactly as the attached image has. No drop shadow, no glow,
no contact shadow below the near edge, nothing at all below the bottom rim. Crisp
edges, not feathered. The whole tile in frame, not cropped, roughly centred, its long
axis exactly horizontal. The tile alone, with nothing standing on or in front of it.
